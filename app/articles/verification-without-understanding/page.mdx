The seven-piece tablebase contains a position in which White forces mate in 549
moves. Every move has been verified as optimal, and the whole line can be printed.
Nobody can say why it wins. Tim Krabbé's verdict on such lines: a grandmaster
understands them no better than someone who learned chess yesterday.

Verified truths that nobody understands used to be rare, because whoever could
not follow one could not have found it either. Machine search changed the supply.
Computer proofs of the four-colour theorem, AlphaZero moves that strong players
cannot explain, and features inside language models that match no human concept
all belong to the same class. This note is about a small, mostly failed attempt to
measure that class in the one domain where the ground truth is complete.

## Three ways of failing to understand

Philosophy has a name for the worry that human understanding has a ceiling. Colin
McGinn (1989) called it *cognitive closure*: a mind M is closed with respect to a
property P when the concept-forming procedures available to M cannot extend to a
grasp of P. Chomsky's "problems versus mysteries" and Nagel's bat make the same
point from different sides. David Deutsch's reply is that a mind with recursive
language, mathematics and external memory is a universal explainer, so nothing
explicable is out of reach in principle.

The debate has stalled for decades, and I think one reason is that both sides treat
"understanding" as something a mind either has or lacks. Vlerick and Boudry (2017)
made the first useful cut. They separate *representational* closure (no true theory
can be represented at all) from *psychological* closure (the theory can be written
down, but not grasped), and argue that McGinn's evidence reaches only the second.

I add one level below theirs and sort the three by where a human fails:

| Level | Where the failure is | Example | Decidable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Intuitive | Cannot picture it, can reason about it formally | 4-D geometry, superposition | Yes, and mathematics already routes around it |
| 2 Capacity | An explanation exists, but its shortest acceptable form exceeds working memory, training time or a lifetime | The 549-move mate | **Measurable** |
| 3 Representational | The concept-forming machinery produces no adequate representation | The ant and the third dimension; perhaps consciousness | Not from the inside |

Level 3 can never be confirmed from inside: a mind missing a concept cannot list
what it is missing. That is both the strength of McGinn's position and the reason
it is hard to argue for. Level 2 is different. "An explanation exists and exceeds a
bound" is a statement about two quantities. If both can be estimated, level 2 stops
being speculation.

So the experiment had one goal: take an endgame and measure the length of its
shortest human-readable explanation.

## Round one: score by optimal moves, and KRK does not calibrate

The plan looked straightforward. Fix a vocabulary of features a chess player
actually uses (king distance, the opposition, the region the defending king can
reach), fit a decision tree to the tablebase's optimal moves, and count the rules
needed for 95% fidelity. The rule count is the explanation length L.

King and rook against king is an ending a club player learns in an hour. The method
could not measure it. Across four endings and three feature vocabularies, no fit
reached 80% agreement with the tablebase's optimal move. KRK topped out at 72% and
stayed there with a thousand rules. Letting the learned rules actually play the
ending out against perfect tablebase defence, within the fifty-move rule, won at
most 19% of games.

The criterion was wrong. Matching the tablebase's move at every step measures the
tablebase's precision, and textbook technique is not move-optimal. The criterion
would classify an ending that every club player understands as incomprehensible.

There was a second problem underneath. I wrote out the textbook KRK method by hand,
five to seven rules, and it converted 30% to 43% of positions. The failures were all
repetitions. Textbooks contain a rule about tempo: when the box cannot shrink, make
a waiting move with the rook. A single-step feature set has no way to say "wait".

## Round two: score by winning, and KRK passes while KBNK is at zero

Switch the criterion to whole-game conversion: the rule list plays White, the
tablebase plays best defence, and only a mate inside fifty moves counts. Let the
rules be an ordered list and let them see a little history (did the last move
shrink the box, is this a repetition).

KRK passed immediately with four rules:

1. do not repeat a position;
2. do not hang the rook or stalemate;
3. bring the king towards the enemy king;
4. otherwise shrink the box.

Three hundred held-out positions, all won; 1,000 fresh positions over three seeds,
99.9% to 100%. Remove any one rule and conversion drops to 42%, 13%, 83% or 0.7%.
Four rules is the order of magnitude of a textbook presentation.

Then bishop and knight against king. This is the ending players find hard. The mate
takes over thirty moves and can only be delivered in a corner of the bishop's
colour, so the defending king runs to the wrong-coloured corner and White has to
escort it along the edge using a fixed knight manoeuvre that traces a W on the
board. With the KRK vocabulary, six rules converted 0% of positions. Adding the one
textbook concept "drive the king towards the bishop's corner" moved that to 0–1%.

The contrast with KRK is the point. **KRK needs only four rules because the concept
"the box" has already done most of the work.** Without it, a tree with hundreds of
leaves could not win in round one. So L was never the length of the explanation. It
was the number of rules *given a concept library*. Change the library and the
number changes.

## Round three: give rules lookahead, reach 87%, stop

Round two failed for a clear reason: the W-manoeuvre is a plan a dozen moves long,
and single-step rules cannot express a plan. Round three borrowed Bratko's 1984
"advice language" for KRK. Each rule names a goal (say, minimise the black king's
distance to the mating corner) and a lookahead depth d, and the engine picks the
move that best advances the goal within d plies against worst-case defence. Depth
counts towards explanation length; otherwise a deep enough search is just the
tablebase again.

KRK gave one clean result: **lookahead trades against rules.** Three rules with
depths (1, 1, 3) won 99.3% of the test set; round two's four rules at depth 1 won
100%. Explanation length can be exchanged between rule count and reasoning depth.

KBNK went from 0% to 87.3% on 300 test positions, with eleven rules and depth at
most 4. A night of beam search on 26 cores, about fifty thousand games per
generation, took the training set to 93% and no further. The pre-registered gate
was 95% on the test set with at most twelve rules. It did not pass.

The sticking point was the same W-manoeuvre. In the wrong corner, pushing the king
out by one square first requires repositioning knight and bishop, and for several
moves the distance-to-corner objective does not change. A 4-ply lookahead sees no
progress and the engine marks time until the fifty-move rule ends the game. Depth
was not even monotone: the same rule list at depths 4, 5 and 6 converted 56%, 25%
and 71% of positions, which is worst-case search behaving differently on odd and
even depths.

## What three rounds show

First, what they do not show. **They do not show that humans are cognitively closed
to KBNK.** Players learn it; a textbook covers it in a page. Three rounds failed to
measure it, and that is a fact about the ruler.

They do show three things.

**Explanation length is always relative to a concept library.** With "the box", KRK
is four rules; without it, hundreds of rules do not win. The same ending looks like
level 3 (every compression fails) when a concept is missing and drops to level 2 (a
short explanation exists) when it is supplied. The boundary between levels 2 and 3
is a property of the library, not of the world. That is an empirical version of the
phrase McGinn's definition turns on, "concept-forming procedures": what decides
closure in practice is not the abstract power of the procedures but which concepts
they have produced so far.

**The rule language decides what can be written short.** KBNK went 0% → 56% → 87%
across the three rounds, and each jump came from extending the language: history,
then goals, then lookahead. The remaining 13% sits in a fixed multi-move sequence
that "rules plus shallow lookahead" cannot compress. How do human players handle
it? They memorise it. The W-manoeuvre has a name in the textbooks; it is a macro.

**A measurement of human limits must first calibrate on things humans understand.**
Round one failed on KRK, which means it was not measuring understanding at all.
This sounds obvious. In discussions of AI producing results humans cannot follow,
the step is usually skipped.

## If this restarts

The entry point is clear: allow move macros in the rule language and charge their
length to the explanation. The cost convention has to come first (an eight-move
macro costs eight, or one?), then KBNK has to clear 95%, and only then is there any
business pushing to five- and seven-piece endings. The curve I wanted, L against
human capacity from KRK out to the 549-move mate, is still the figure I would like
to see. I now know that drawing it first requires proving the ruler is straight.

The point of the exercise was to split an uncomfortable question into a part that
can be measured and a part that is marked undecidable. The experiment did not give
me the figure. It gave me a precise account of why the ruler bends, which is worth
more than a figure that does not hold up.
