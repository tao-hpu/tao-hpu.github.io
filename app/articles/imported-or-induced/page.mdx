> **About the versions.** This note accompanies version 2 of the paper,
> [Imported or Induced?](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21869829). Version 1 had a
> different title, *Imported Verdicts: Where Language Model Errors Land in a Fully
> Fictional Legal Domain*, and the opposite conclusion. It remains in the Zenodo version
> history; the DOI above resolves to the current version.

Fictional-domain evaluations give a model rules that differ from the real world and
watch what it does with them. When the model gets an item wrong, the wrong answer is
often the one the real world would give, and that is usually read as the model importing
what it already knows. I built a testbed to measure this for law, ran 18 models on it,
and in version 1 of this paper I reported that they import real legal doctrine. The
errors landed on the doctrine option well above a third of the time, and most of all at
a few conflict sites where the stronger models erred.

Version 2 reports what happened when I controlled the design of the testbed itself.
Three choices I had made could each produce the import signal without any import. With
all three controlled, the share of errors landing on doctrine is at chance, and the
doctrine test I preregistered for the last follow-up failed. Two results remain that the
controls do not explain. This note walks through the three choices with the data that
undid each one.

## The testbed

The testbed is twelve fictional Chinese statutes, three to five clauses each, in an
invented legal vocabulary. Eight of them mirror a well-known doctrine (good-faith
acquisition, consideration, proportional self-defense, strict liability, possessory
liens, limitation, negotiorum gestio, lost property) but stipulate a different outcome
at one pivot clause. A scan for 39 real legal terms across all rules, cases and options
finds none.

Each statute compiles to a function over the case's features, and every stored label is
that function's output, so no label is written by hand or by a model. Items are
four-option cases. On divergent items the stipulated verdict and the doctrine verdict
differ, and the doctrine option is the "import target." On aligned items the two
coincide, and accuracy there is the competence axis, cut into floor (below .40), mid
(.40 to .80) and high (.80 and above) bands.

The measure is the doctrine landing share: of a set of errors, the fraction that chose
the import target. Because the rules are executable, I can enumerate for every item
which verdicts the rules can produce and which single misreading of the case reaches
each wrong option. That enumeration is what took version 1 apart.

## First choice: which chance to compare against

Import targets rotate over the three wrong positions, so a model that ignores content
lands on the target a third of the time. That was my baseline in version 1, and against
it the 18 models show a competence curve: 41% of floor-band errors land on doctrine, 48%
of mid-band errors and 34% of high-band errors, pooled over seven targets. Read at face
value, import rises and then falls as models get better.

A third is the wrong chance. Each rule set's function returns only two to four of the
four option verdicts, and most errors stay among the verdicts the rules can produce. On
liens, limitation and negotiorum gestio, the doctrine verdict is the only producible
wrong option, so any model that prefers producible verdicts lands on it above a third.
On good-faith acquisition and self-defense the rules never produce it, and errors land
there 26 of 99, 12 of 90 and 0 of 36 times across the three bands. A null that conditions
on producible verdicts, calibrated on aligned-item errors, predicts the observed counts
in all three bands, and a mixed model with that offset puts every band at the null.

This null depends on how one clause is read. Limitation clause Q5, which the function
does not model, can be read literally to produce one more option, and under that reading
the mid band shows a modest excess (exact P = .014; mixed model one-sided p = .098). The
paper reports both readings, and the checklist asks for the same.

## Second choice: one sentence in the case

After the first run, two conflict sites held most of the high-band errors. At a
lost-property site all 25 errors landed on doctrine; at a strict-liability site, 22 of
24. I wrote new items for both sites and the pattern held: 91% (77 of 85) and 95% (82 of
86). This was the core of version 1's claim, because it was the strong models erring
toward the real rule.

Each site's case text contained a sentence that neither null looked at. Every
lost-property case said the prior owner "named the treasure's private mark exactly."
Under two clauses of the same statute, naming the mark returns the object to its owner
in a market setting. A model that stretches that clause to the case reaches the doctrine
verdict using only the fictional rules. Every strict-liability case said the victim
"intruded into the work site," which triggers the clause that halves liability. Once a
model wrongly finds the operator liable, that clause sends it to halved liability, which
is also the doctrine verdict. For strict liability the doctrine verdict was the only
wrong verdict a single misreading could reach, so the 95% carried no information about
import.

The two preregistered follow-ups deleted each sentence and kept everything else.

At lost property, 66 of 74 high-band errors land on doctrine with the sentence (89%,
route null 1/2) and 20 of 44 without it (45%, null 0.4, P = .28). The difference in
excess over each arm's null is 4.21 log-odds (SE 1.22). I had preregistered that the
de-cued errors would still exceed their null; that hypothesis failed.

At strict liability, deleting "intruded into the work site" makes full liability the
doctrine verdict. High-band doctrine answers on conflict items fall from 108 to 57
(paired McNemar 81 vs. 30, one-sided p = 6.9 × 10⁻⁷), and mid-band ones from 70 to 0.
The remaining 57 high-band errors are not at chance, though. All 57 choose full
liability, the new doctrine verdict. I read the stated reasons for all of them: each
invokes fault, and 53 cite clause F5 ("the delegate's fault counts as the operator's
fault"), reading it as creating fault rather than transferring it. That route was not
in my preregistered list of misreadings. Counting it raises the null to 2/3, and 57 of
57 still exceeds it. The reasons cite no doctrine, but the reading is post hoc, and the
halved-liability option is also the longest option in both arms. This is the first of
the two residuals.

## Third choice: which clause comes last

The first follow-up also built three new conflict sites where each wrong option was
reachable by exactly one misreading of each kind, so the route null was 1/3 per item.
The preregistered test failed: 29 of 66 high-band errors on doctrine, expected 22.0,
P = .047 against a required .01. Nearly all the excess was at one site (thrown objects),
and 26 of its 28 doctrine landings came from Qwen2.5-7B. Its stated reasons applied the
third and last exception as a catch-all. On twin items, where the last exception gives a
non-doctrine verdict, all 34 of its errors landed there. At the deposit site the
doctrinal exception was listed first and drew 0 of 18 errors. I had rotated the
doctrinal exception across sites, which confounded clause position with site.

The second follow-up rendered each of 24 new surfaces per site three times, rotating the
three exceptions in a Latin square, with case text and option order identical across the
versions.

Errors follow the last-listed exception: 187 of 319 high-band errors land on it where it
is not the doctrinal one (59%, expected 106.3). Qwen2.5-7B (75 of 84) and Qwen2.5-3B
(108 of 229) carry the effect, and they differ in form. A conditional logit finds
Qwen2.5-7B favoring the third exception, Qwen2.5-3B the second and third, and the
mid-band gemma-2-2b the second.

With position counterbalanced, high-band conflict errors land on doctrine 43 of 134
times, against 44.7 expected (P = .65). Qwen2.5-7B lands on doctrine 1 of 8, 1 of 3 and
17 of 17 times as the doctrinal exception moves from first to last. Entered together in
a conditional logit, last position gets a coefficient of 1.27 (p = .010) and doctrinal
identity −0.29 (p = .36). The preregistered doctrine hypothesis failed.

What this result can bound matters. All 134 errors come from those two models. Treated
as independent, the 95% interval for the doctrine share is 24% to 41%, and the test had
power .80 only for excesses of 11 points or more. It says nothing about import in other
models.

The second residual sits here. Qwen2.5-7B's own conditional logit gives doctrinal
identity a coefficient of 1.80 (uncorrected p = .019), identified mainly by the 11
conflict errors where the doctrinal exception was not last. On those 11 it lands on
doctrine twice (P = .92).

## What twins do and do not show

The control I relied on in version 1 was the matched twin: repeat a conflict item's
case text and flip the conflicting clause so that the stipulated verdict agrees with
doctrine. In the amplified suite, twins cut high-band error rates from 27% to 2% and
10%, and I read that as tying the errors to the doctrine conflict. But flipping the
clause also makes the doctrine answer correct. Twins measure whether a conflict adds
errors, not where the added errors go. On the cue-balanced sites of the later suites,
twins draw more errors than conflict items (110 vs. 40 on deposits, 116 vs. 67 on
thrown objects).

The same amplified suite had a labeling problem that the paper documents in full. Its
generator first wrote the strict-liability import target as full liability, against the
frozen halved-liability target from the first run. I corrected it after nine of the 12
models, and most of a tenth, had run. Under the run-time label, high-band landing on
that block is 4 of 86; under the corrected label, 82 of 86. I found it only because the
solver copied the run-time label into every result row, so the paper treats that block
as descriptive only. In the last follow-up every row records its run-time label, and
none of the 10,560 differs from the frozen one.

## How common these checks are

I coded 44 published evaluations that read prior-consistent answers as knowledge import,
on four checks. Two state a chance model for where errors land. Ten check whether item or
context cues could make the real-world answer correct or attractive. One of the 28 with
orderable options or evidence counterbalances order and reports the effect. Twin or
aligned controls are the common one, at 26 of 44. No paper does the first three together,
and 32 do none of them. A random fifth was recoded independently, with agreement on 31 of
36 decisions (κ = 0.80). The checks were derived from the artifacts in this paper, so the
audit measures how often published work runs these particular checks, and 25 of the 44
use free-form answers, where a chance model for error location is harder to define.

The paper closes with a six-item checklist: derive the null from the verdicts the rules
can produce, enumerate single-misreading routes and add a de-cued arm when the real-world
answer is the only reachable one, read a sample of raw outputs for routes the null does
not list, rotate clause and option positions and report position preference per model,
use twins for error volume rather than destination, and freeze materials by hash with
run-time labels logged per row. Reading raw outputs is on the list because both
follow-ups found a misreading route, the catch-all last exception and the F5 reading,
that my enumerated null did not model.

## What I now think

These data do not show doctrine-specific import at conflict sites once the three choices
are controlled. They do not show that models never import doctrine. The three frontier
models in the follow-ups (claude-sonnet-4.5, gpt-4o, gpt-5) made one conflict-item error
between them, so the testbed cannot speak to them at all. The open-weights models are
small (0.5B to 9B) and mostly from one family, the domain is one fictional legal system
in one language, and each artifact is shown on few sites. The follow-up freezes are
hashed archives with local timestamps, not third-party registrations, and the second
follow-up was designed after the first one's results.

What changed for me is the step from "errors land on the real-world answer" to "the
model imported the real world." Each time I took that step, a feature of my own items
had already put the real-world answer one misreading away, or listed it last. An
executable rule set makes that checkable per item.
