People who use chat models in Chinese often say the answers read like translated English:
European sentence shapes, pronouns where Chinese would drop them, English punctuation. The
stage that usually gets the blame is RLHF. Preference optimization, the story goes, rewards
one house style whose center of mass sits in English, and every other language is pulled
toward it. As far as I know, nobody had tested it stage by stage.
The paper behind this note,
[Supervised Finetuning Sets Cross-Language Representation Geometry; LLM Judge Scores Fall Further Off the Axis](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23718991),
is that test.

The short version has two halves that point in different directions. On the representation
side, the Chinese–English convergence enters at supervised finetuning, and the later stages
barely move it. On the output side, LLM judges score Chinese chat answers lower after SFT,
but the two native speakers who rated a paired sample did not agree with each other, so I
cannot say that people perceive a loss of naturalness. Most of this note is about keeping
those two halves apart.

## Two open ladders and one fixed ruler

The test needs a post-training pipeline whose intermediate checkpoints are public. Two
exist. Tülu&nbsp;3 releases Llama-3.1-8B, its SFT model, its DPO model and its final RLVR
model as separate checkpoints. OLMo&nbsp;2&nbsp;7B does the same on a base model whose
pretraining corpus is heavily English-centric. The pair gives a contrast in pretraining
language balance under one family of recipes. Neither ladder contains classical RLHF (PPO
against a human-preference reward model), so the comparison is SFT against DPO and RLVR,
and PPO-based RLHF stays untested.

The ruler is a register axis of the kind I used in the
[workspace-registers working paper](/articles/workspace-registers), with the same bilingual
pools. For each layer it is the difference of means between the residual representations of
16 content-matched English–Chinese sentence pairs, computed on the **base model only** and
then held fixed for every later checkpoint. Sixty bilingual probe texts, topically disjoint
from the axis pairs, are fed as bare text with no chat template, so each checkpoint sees
byte-identical input. The summary numbers average over layers 8–23, the central half of the
32-layer stack. The quantity I track is the gap between the mean English and mean Chinese
positions on the axis.

## The gap closes once

On Tülu&nbsp;3 the gap falls from 3.975 to 3.468 at SFT, a 12.8% contraction. On
OLMo&nbsp;2 it falls from 6.187 to 4.952, 20.0%. After that it does not keep closing. From
SFT to the final model the gap moves by 0.017 and 0.030 in the opposite direction, a slight
re-widening worth 3.3% and 2.4% of the SFT effect. All stages share the same bootstrap
resample in each draw, so these are paired differences with intervals: the SFT contraction
is 0.507 [0.447, 0.534] on Tülu and 1.235 [1.104, 1.305] on OLMo.

The direction is also set once. The SFT increment has cosine 0.943 (Tülu) and 0.962 (OLMo)
with the total base-to-final drift of the Chinese items. The DPO and RLVR increments are a
third or less of the SFT norm (on OLMo the RLVR step is 1/29 of it), and their cosines with
the total drift are 0.06 to 0.23. The preference stages do change the representation, but
mostly in directions other than this axis. On Tülu, DPO moves both languages along the axis
(Chinese +0.358 to +0.244, English −0.149 to −0.250) while changing the gap between them by
0.013.

One qualification: this holds for the middle of the network. In the last three layers DPO
does add contraction. At the final hidden-state index on Tülu the contraction is 5.1% after
SFT, 20.8% after DPO and 21.5% after RLVR. Whether that late-layer change has anything to
do with the output-side decline below, I have not tested.

## Which language moves depends on the base

The two families agree on the stage and disagree on the geometry. Toggle the figure. On
Tülu&nbsp;3 both languages move toward each other, roughly a 70/30 split: Chinese +0.358,
English −0.149. On OLMo&nbsp;2 Chinese moves +1.385 while English moves +0.150, slightly
*away* from Chinese, so the whole contraction is Chinese moving toward English. My reading
is that post-training pulls both languages toward one assistant register whose location is
inherited from pretraining. On a balanced base it sits between the languages; on an
English-centric base it sits at the English pole.

There is a confound I cannot remove. OLMo&nbsp;2's base model handles Chinese poorly: 32 of
its 60 bare Chinese continuations are degenerate. Part of the one-sided movement may be
Chinese processing collapsing toward English rather than a change of register.

## Is it register at all?

An axis built from cross-language pairs carries language identity by construction. A
shrinking gap could then mean the benign thing, better cross-lingual alignment, rather than
a change of style. So on each base model I built a language-identity axis from 30 English
and 30 Chinese texts with *no* shared content, so that only the language signal survives the
average, plus a within-Chinese translationese axis from 16 pairs that state the same
proposition idiomatically and with Europeanized syntax.

The register axis is mostly language: its cosine with the identity axis is 0.72 on Tülu and
0.79 on OLMo. The contraction appears on both axes. The test is what remains after removing
the identity component. On OLMo the residual axis still contracts by 34% at SFT, so the
one-sided convergence is not just language-identity collapse. On Tülu the residual
contraction is 0.9%, so the symmetric convergence is almost entirely language-collinear,
consistent with the benign reading. And on the translationese axis, the SFT drift of the
Chinese items moves slightly toward the *idiomatic* pole on both families. Whatever SFT
writes into the Chinese representations, it is not translationese in this sense.

## What the judges see, and what two people see

On the output side I generated from every checkpoint with greedy decoding on 60
content-matched Chinese/English prompts. Chat outputs come from each instruct stage's own
template; a 2-shot bare condition runs on all four stages so that base anchors the ladder.

Fed back through the base model, the generated text tells a different story from the
probes. The gap between English and Chinese output narrows mainly because *English* output
drifts toward the middle (Tülu bare +1.33 to +1.11) while Chinese output barely moves.
Assistant English is register-marked English.

The LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.6, temperature 0, blind to stage) records a Chinese decline.
Chat naturalness on a 1–5 scale goes 3.39, 3.13, 3.05 across SFT, DPO and RLVR on Tülu, and
2.82, 2.66, 2.46 on OLMo. On items scored at all three stages, the paired SFT-to-final
difference is −0.38 [−0.68, −0.11] on OLMo and −0.24 [−0.55, +0.07] on Tülu. A GPT-5.4
judge rates higher overall and reproduces the decline (−0.54 and −0.46). Judged English
naturalness stays between 4.67 and 4.95 at every stage.

Two surface features rise after SFT on both families: the share of lines that are list or
markdown items (0.34 to 0.49 on Tülu, 0.36 to 0.48 on OLMo) and the share of Latin letters
(0.2% to 1.2%, 0.1% to 2.3%). Holding their paired changes fixed by regression accounts for
25–52% of the judged decline. Western punctuation, the marker I would have bet on after the
[punctuation-staircase note](/articles/punctuation-staircase), does not rise within chat:
8.8 to 8.1 per thousand characters on Tülu, 7.2 to 7.7 on OLMo. Lists are the common thread
with that note, where list layout turned out to be what separated model replies from human
text.

Then the human anchor. Two native speakers rated the SFT and RLVR chat outputs of 50
prompts, shown without stage, family or judge score. Rater A is a volunteer and the primary
human evidence. Rater B is me: I knew the hypothesis, I generated the sample files, I rated
at a median of 4 seconds per item, and my test-retest correlation was 0.19, so my ratings
are the weakest evidence in the paper. The two raters agree with each other at
Spearman &rho;&nbsp;=&nbsp;0.08, and each agrees with the primary judge at 0.15–0.16. On
OLMo the paired RLVR-minus-SFT difference is −0.43 [−0.77, −0.10] under the judge and
+0.13 [−0.33, +0.60] for rater A. Neither rater's change is significant.

The disagreement has a visible source. Rater A's scores rise with list lines and Latin
script; the judges' scores fall with Latin script, and so do mine. Whether native readers
count those features as unnatural is exactly what the two raters disagree on. So the
judged decline is real as a property of the judges, and a human-perceived loss of
naturalness is not established. Fifty prompts and two raters, one of them the author,
cannot rule one out either.

## Subtracting the direction

If the direction SFT moves were what makes the output read as translated, removing it
should help. I tested this on the Tülu&nbsp;3 final model with inference-time hooks on
layers 8–23.

<DriftSubtraction />

Subtracting half the mean Chinese drift vector restores 42% of the gap that SFT closed
(3.485 to 3.692, against the base 3.975), with no degenerate English outputs and flat
usefulness. Same-norm random directions at the same layers and dose restore none of it. So
the measured direction is causally connected to the representation gap. Judged naturalness
does not come back: it is 3.63 against 4.11 with no edit, a paired difference of −0.51
[−0.75, −0.27]. Doubling the dose collapses generation; all 60 Chinese outputs loop.

Put next to the observational result, both lines agree: the low-rank direction SFT moves
governs the representation gap, and the judged change lives somewhere else. My conjecture
is that it is a property of the generation policy rather than of this direction. The
evidence is one symmetric edit at one working dose on one family, scored by an LLM judge.
A symmetric edit is also limited by design, since restoring a between-language gap
means moving one language relative to the other; language-conditional and weight-space
edits are the next tests.

## Japanese

I wrote content-matched Japanese versions of all three pools and reran the ladder on both
families. Every representation-side signature repeats. The gap contracts at SFT by 0.408
(Tülu) and 1.028 (OLMo), and the post-SFT residual is 5.2% and 3.8% of that. The SFT
increment has cosine 0.93 and 0.96 with the total drift. The geometry splits the same way:
Japanese +0.263 and English −0.145 on Tülu, Japanese +1.151 and English +0.122 on OLMo.

A single judge scores Japanese chat output below Chinese on both families (Tülu 3.07, 2.88,
2.82 across the instruct stages). OLMo Japanese sits near the floor from SFT onward, with
85% of final-stage answers rated heavy translationese, so a further decline could not show
in the mean. These are uncalibrated score levels with no human anchor, and the
language-identity control has not been repeated for Japanese.

## What I take from it

On the representation side the attribution holds within its scope: in the middle layers,
the Chinese–English convergence on this axis enters at SFT, and DPO and RLVR leave it where
SFT put it. Which language moves, and whether the movement is register or language identity,
depends on the pretraining balance of the base model. If non-English register is the
concern, the SFT mixture and pretraining balance are the places to look before preference
data.

The behavioral form of the folk claim stays open. Judges see a continued decline after SFT,
off the axis; two people do not confirm it and disagree about the features driving it.
Settling that needs a larger rater panel, or calibration against existing human
naturalness data.

The limits are the ones above plus a few structural ones. Both recipes are AI2's. Both
non-English languages are high-resource CJK languages. The axis is one direction per layer
and carries a large language-identity component. The representation numbers describe bare
text, not the deployed chat condition, which the output side covers. The attribution is to
stages as released, and in Tülu&nbsp;3 the preference stages use less data and compute than
SFT; their increments are also mostly off the SFT direction, so more of that optimization
would plausibly move the representation elsewhere rather than further along the axis.
