Ask a Chinese AI search product whether a rice cooker is worth buying and it answers in
prose, citing a handful of pages. Those citations are the answer's evidence. If a place
on a cited page can be bought, part of the answer can be bought too. The practice has a
name, generative engine optimization (GEO), and in China it has a visible market:
placement on content platforms is sold per domain, and some offers advertise "GEO" or
"AI inclusion" as a feature.

Work on detecting this kind of manipulation usually treats the manipulation itself as
unobservable and relies on a proxy, most often the share of an answer's citations that
land on low-authority domains. That proxy has not been tested against a control group
that asks the same kind of question and differs only in commercial motive. My paper,
[*CGEP: Pairing Chinese Generative-Search Citation Chains with an Open Paid-Placement
Market*](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23719633), builds that test. It releases a two-part resource
([dataset DOI](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22084160)): real generative-search answers
joined to the supply side of the placement market (CGEP-ITW), and a synthetic benchmark
that comes with an audit of its own construction (CGEP-Synth).

## A control that asks the same question

The unit of observation is a **citation group**: all pages cited by one answer. I
sampled 62 entities in seven strata and asked two questions about each, "is X worth
buying" and "X or Y, which is better", for 124 answers collected on 11 July 2026. They
contain 1,777 citations over 768 distinct pages on 334 hosts; 684 of those pages were
fetched as snapshots.

Four strata carry commercial motive: small appliances and electronics (A), education
and certification (B), medical aesthetics and health products (C), and major brands
(D). The control that carries the design is **E1**: seven low-ticket household goods,
a drying rack or a toilet brush, asked with the same two questions. Relative to the
treatment strata the question type stays fixed and one thing changes: whether buying
placement would pay. A factual-question control would change the question type as well
as the motive. E2 (three factual entities) and Dcal (two non-Chinese brands) serve as
floors.

The supply side is a paid-placement price list received unsolicited in July 2026:
41,443 offers across 11,355 domains, of which 816 offers on 204 domains are sold
explicitly in terms of generative retrieval, at a median price of 18 RMB. Nothing was
bought or negotiated. The raw list is an operational directory of placement channels,
so it is withheld as dual-use; the release contains only derived statistics
(domain-level overlap counts, price quantiles, GEO-flag counts), with overlapping
domains pseudonymized.

Each group is coded on seven signals tied to a five-technique taxonomy (below). Two
carry the result. **S1** fires when at least half the citations sit on domains outside
the Tranco top 100k: the low-authority proxy. **S7** fires when a cited host appears
in the price list, a table lookup with no coder judgment.

## Low authority does not discriminate; purchasability does

Start on S1. It fires on 25 of 100 treatment groups and 8 of 24 controls, an odds
ratio of 0.67 in the wrong direction and not significant (p&nbsp;=&nbsp;0.86; on the
continuous share, 0.325 against 0.351, p&nbsp;=&nbsp;0.53). Switch to "by stratum" to
see why. E1 trips S1 on 8 of 14 groups: a drying rack is answered from small retailer
and manufacturer pages whether or not anyone paid for them. Major brands (D) trip it on
2 of 20, because they buy placement on popular hosts. Popularity measures salience,
and salience is only incidentally related to manipulation.

Now S7. Three quarters of treatment groups cite at least one purchasable host (75 of
100) against a quarter of controls (6 of 24), odds ratio 9.00,
p&nbsp;=&nbsp;8.4&nbsp;&times;&nbsp;10<sup>&minus;6</sup>. Restricting to offers sold
explicitly as GEO raises it to 11.67. The obvious objection is that Chinese AI search
leans on a few large publishing platforms that appear in any placement catalogue;
three such hosts account for 151 of the 245 cited pages that overlap the list.
Deleting every page on the twelve highest-traffic platforms leaves 66 of 449 treatment
pages (14.7%) against 3 of 143 control pages (2.1%), odds ratio 8.04,
p&nbsp;=&nbsp;3.9&nbsp;&times;&nbsp;10<sup>&minus;6</sup>. The gap does not come from
the portals.

Stratum D is the case a single detector misses. Its S1 rate is the lowest in the
sample and its S7 rate is 12 of 20, every hit on an explicit GEO offer. Across the
cross-tabulation, the most common cell in the commercial strata is S1 absent and S7
present: 23 of 30 groups in A, 17 of 20 in C, 12 of 20 in D. Content placed on popular
hosts is invisible to a detector that equates manipulation with obscurity.

A note on method. An earlier version of the coder called any host missing from a
hand-built whitelist low-authority. On the full sample it classed the China
Meteorological Administration and a Chinese Academy of Sciences institute as marketing
sites and fired on all 20 groups in E1 and E2. The current definition uses a public
ranking (Tranco list N2Q7W, threshold fixed before analysis). The release ships the
rank subset for the 288 cited domains rather than the list.

## The taxonomy, and the technique that never appeared

The paper sorts manipulation on two axes: the channel through which the engine is
influenced (retrieval corpus, runtime session, or authority signal) and the
organizational form (single item or coordinated cluster). Five techniques result:

- **T1, account-matrix farming**: many accounts run by one party publish same-leaning
  content.
- **T2, citation stuffing**: pages cite one another to manufacture authority.
- **T3, parasite content**: commercial content on a popular, trusted host.
- **T4, hidden injection**: page content addressed to the model rather than the reader.
- **T5, fake-review clusters**: coordinated reviews across platforms.

The taxonomy is descriptive, drawn from public enforcement reporting and from the
offer categories in the price list. The defender's task splits into detection (is this
chain manipulated), classification (which technique), and attribution (which actor
cluster placed it). CGEP-ITW supports only the first two, and only as observability,
because no ground truth about transactions exists in the wild.

One cell stayed empty. No LLM-directed hidden instruction appeared in any of the 684
snapshots, so S5, the T4 signal, is zero throughout. On the synthetic benchmark the
same technique is perfectly separable because a boolean flag carries it. The easiest
technique on constructed data is the one I could not observe on real pages.

## A synthetic benchmark that audits itself

CGEP-Synth generates campaigns over cluster sizes 3, 10 and 30 crossed with three
levels of intra-cluster homogeneity, in three realizations up to all five techniques
(7,740 items, 774 per technique, balanced against organic items), each under five
seeds. Items carry accounts, sellers, platforms, scalar and framing features, and edges
in an interaction graph and a citation graph. It contains no natural-language text.

Its organizing thesis is that each technique hides coordination in a different
substrate, so a detector on the wrong substrate fails. That thesis is built into the
generator, and the paper states how much of the resulting separability follows from
construction:

- Of the twelve technique-by-substrate cells, eight are structural zeros: the objects a
  detector needs are absent from that substrate, so 0.00 reflects an empty graph, not a
  failed model. All 1,501 interaction edges lie inside T1 clusters.
- Two more are near-perfect because a label is readable as a feature. The DOM-carrier
  flag is true for exactly the 774 T4 items. Host authority is a constant 0.300 for four
  techniques, while T3 draws from 0.701 to 0.950 and organic items from 0.2 to 0.6.
- Only content similarity on T3 (F1 0.547) and T5 (0.351) is earned by modeling, and
  there the classes overlap.

Content features detect coordination well (F1 0.934) but attribute it to a seller

under one-to-one Hungarian assignment (a majority-mapping variant inflates every graph
method to 1.00). A graph autoencoder does not beat the classical baseline. These
numbers say a method can or cannot recover a structure the generator planted. Claims
about real accounts have to come from somewhere else, which is the reason CGEP-ITW
exists.

## Evidence strength and boundaries

**CGEP-Synth is synthetic and mostly decided by construction.** Ten of its twelve
technique-by-substrate cells are fixed by the generator. It supports comparing
attribution methods, not statements about real sellers.

**CGEP-ITW is observational and small.** It is one instrument at one point in time,
and generative-search citation behavior shifts over months. The instrument is a
search-grounded LLM API with live retrieval over Chinese pages, not the consumer apps
of Chinese engines, which need authenticated sessions; the bare chat APIs of those
vendors do not browse and, in pilot testing, invented source URLs. The dataset shows
how the Chinese content ecosystem appears in a generative-search citation chain, not
how a particular product behaves. Coding is by one author with no agreement statistic;
S7, as a lookup, needs none. Signals S2, S3 and S6 are often unverifiable at this
sample size and support no conclusion. The price list is one vendor's catalogue and
says nothing about market size. E2 and Dcal give a floor, not a rate.

**What S7 does not show.** S7 records that a cited host *can* be bought, not that any
citation *was* bought. No claim attaches a transaction to a page, entity, or account,
and no entity is named as a manipulator. Confounding remains: user-publishing
platforms take 5.2% of citations in E1 against 34.1% in stratum A, so the composition
of the citation surface itself varies with commercial motive. The defensible statement
is that commercially motivated queries draw on a citation surface that overlaps an open
placement market far more than motive-matched controls do.

## What I take from it

For anyone using synthetic manipulation corpora: for each attack class, write down
which feature channel carries it and whether that channel is populated for the other
classes. Where it is not, a reported zero is a property of the generator. The check is
cheap, and once it is done the point cannot be argued afterwards.

For detection practice: popularity rankings are convenient weak supervision, and in
this sample they point the wrong way. A supply-side lookup is cheaper, needs no
judgment, and here carried an odds ratio of 9.00 against 0.67. Its cost is that it
identifies exposure, not manipulation, and it exists only where a placement market can
be observed.
