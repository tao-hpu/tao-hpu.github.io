> **Rewritten 28 October 2026.** This note now follows the revised working paper, *Output
> Anticipation or Internal Content? Auditing Jacobian-Lens Readouts with Mouth-Exclusion*.
> The earlier version, written in July for a draft titled *Registers, Not Plans*, read the
> audit below as evidence that the workspace holds context registers and not content plans.
> Two positive controls added since then do not support that reading. A trained probe reads
> the upcoming rhyme where the lens reads nothing, but never beats a baseline that reads no
> activations; an entity placed in three different roles is filtered out in all three. The
> audit numbers and the language-steering result carry over. The interpretation is
> narrower, and the self-report and transport-geometry sections are gone.

Anthropic's interpretability team proposed that a language model keeps intermediate "thoughts" in a global workspace: a residual-stream subspace that a Jacobian lens can read and that swaps and steering can edit ([Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/workspace/index.html)). The lens is the network's own derivative of its final hidden state with respect to an intermediate layer, averaged over a corpus and decoded through the unembedding: a training-free relative of the tuned lens.

I replicated the core experiments on open models, Qwen3-1.7B and 4B with the official pre-fitted lenses, and Gemma-2-2B as a second family. Some of it replicates. On 4B, swapping a country's direction moves the full pattern of facts about it in 35 of 36 items; the same swap does almost nothing for months, animals or numbers (0/12, 1/14, 0/12). Rhyme planning reads at 0% at the designated position, and mental arithmetic sits at its permutation floor (7.3% against 7.3% on 1.7B).

That mixed picture raised the question this note is about. Intermediate layers of a trained decoder increasingly anticipate the output; that has been known since the logit lens. A lens readout that recovers a token the model was about to emit anyway is evidence of that convergence, not of an internal representation. So which lens readouts show something the model is *not* about to say?

## Filtering out the next token

For every lens hit I also record the decoded token's rank in the model's actual next-token distribution at the same position, which I call the *mouth* for short. A hit whose token sits high in the mouth may be next-token anticipation. A hit counts as *covert* under the strict criterion when the token is in the lens's top 10 and at mouth rank 100 or worse. The original paper used a version of this once, exempting the top 10 tokens of a clean pass; I make it the main instrument and call it mouth-exclusion. Permutation controls, which re-pair items with other items' targets, give a false-positive floor for each set under identical scoring.

One property of the filter matters for everything that follows: it matches exact tokens at the next position only. It does not remove anticipation several tokens ahead, or anticipation of the same word in another language or spelling.

## Two of six sets clear their floors

<RegisterPlanBars />

Under the strict criterion, four of the original paper's six evaluation sets fall near their permutation floors on Qwen: poetry (rhyme), order of operations, association, and multi-hop bridge entities. The two that clear them are multilingual (31.3% on 4B against a 3.3% floor) and typo correction (15.6% against 0%). Against their own permutation controls, those two give Holm-adjusted one-sided Fisher tests at p ≤ 2×10⁻⁴ on both models; none of the four content sets reaches significance after adjustment.

What the two surviving sets share is a surface form the model is not about to emit. Multilingual items prompt in another language, and their listed intermediates are mostly English: the relation word, the English form of the input word, the English form of the answer. Typo items list the correct spelling of a word the prompt misspells. The four failing sets target content in the form the model will produce, and their lens hits cluster just outside the mouth's top 10, which is where output anticipation would put them.

The slot split narrows the multilingual result further. The language name, the one slot that is a fact about the context rather than content, clears its floor on only 6.5 to 7.5% of items. Most surviving hits are English content words, which matches the English-leaning intermediate layers reported for multilingual models by [Wendler et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10588). The next token at the readout position is the answer in the prompt's language, so a lens hit on its English form may be anticipation of the answer in a form the exact-token filter cannot match. The stored ranks cannot test this.

## Why the audit alone does not settle planning

My July reading of this split was that the workspace holds context registers, not content plans. That reading has a hole. A plan, meaning content held for later output, can itself raise the next-token probability of that content. A filter that removes output-adjacent hits is then biased against the very representation it is supposed to test, and calling the surviving sets "registers" and the failing ones "absent plans" is a definition, not a finding. Two positive controls test what the filter can and cannot show.

## A probe at the rhyme position

The poetry set asks whether the rhyme word that will end line 2 is readable at the newline that ends line 1. The lens reads nothing there. So for every fitted layer I train a linear map on the residual at that newline, initialised at the identity so training starts from the logit lens, with the rhyme word the model itself goes on to produce as target. Training couplets are the model's own: sampled first lines, greedy second lines, kept only if the two last words rhyme under the CMU pronouncing dictionary. Evaluation is on held-out generated couplets and on the official 98 couplets, which are never trained on.

Next to the probe I put a baseline that reads no activations at all. It ranks rhyme words by how often they appear in the rhyme class of line 1's last word in the model's own training couplets, then by overall frequency.

<RhymeProbeControl />

The probe reads what the lens misses: 49 to 81% pass@10 on held-out couplets against 0 to 1% for the lens and 7 to 12% for the probe's permutation control. Every probe hit is covert by my own criterion, since the rhyme word's median mouth rank at the newline is above 15,000 on every model. The lens's silence at this position is a fact about the lens, not about the residual stream.

But the frequency baseline beats the probe on every model and every split (94.9% against 81.4% on 1.7B held-out). Within the rhyme class, where the question is which of 87 to 157 candidates the model will pick, no fitted layer of any model beats the baseline either. The selected layers are early (layer 0 on 1.7B, layer 5 on Gemma, layer 20 on 4B), and moving the frozen probe back to line 1's last word shows the per-item readout is already there. A representation of a chosen word would have to beat a reader that knows only the rhyme class. On these models the probe reads the constraint the context has already fixed, and finds no evidence of a selection among the words that satisfy it. Tripling the 1.7B training data, from 854 to 2,484 pairs, raised the probe from 69.0% to 81.4% and left the within-class comparison unchanged.

## One entity, three roles

The second control holds content fixed and varies its role, which is the experiment a planning reading needs. Forty-eight entities are each named only by a description that never contains the word ("the desert animal that stores fat in its hump"). Every prompt then contains the same preamble, *Sure, I know this one.*, followed by a stem that differs by arm. In the plan arm the entity is the next token. In the register arm the task is to state a property, such as how many legs it has, without naming it, so the entity conditions the answer and is never emitted. In the idle arm the model changes the subject. Items enter the analysis only if the plan arm actually names the entity, 41 to 46 of 48 depending on the model.

Covert readout is between 0 and 2.8% in every arm on every model, against floors of 0%. Role does not decide it. On 4B and Gemma the lens does read the entity, on 12 to 23% of preamble positions against 0% for a control word, and those hits concentrate at three pause points: the comma, *this*, and the period. At those positions the entity's median mouth rank drops in the plan arm and also in the register arm, where it will never be produced (4B: 88, 66, 83 against 109, 56, 277; elsewhere in the preamble the pooled medians run from 138 to 1,057). The three positions were picked after looking at the lens hits, so this breakdown is exploratory. On 1.7B the lens reads the entity on only 1 to 5% of positions, so its near-zero covert rate reflects low lens sensitivity, not the filter. The small residue on Gemma is larger in the plan arm (2.8%) than in the register arm (1.6%), the opposite of what a register-over-plan split predicts.

So the filter does remove activated content that is not about to be said, but not selectively: a computed entity reaches the next-token distribution at pause points whether it is scheduled for output, held for a judgement, or about to be dropped. The multilingual and typo targets pass for a different reason. They never appear in the form the model is about to emit, so they pass whether or not they are anticipation. Neither case lets the audit separate planned content from unplanned.

## Writing along a language axis

Reading tests whether the lens can see something; writing tests whether the model uses it. I call a variable that conditions every output token but is never emitted a *register*; the language of the conversation is the obvious example. I estimate a language axis per band layer as the mass-mean contrast between 16 English/Chinese parallel sentences, disjoint from the test items, and shift the residual along it by a fraction α of the measured population gap. The control shifts by the same per-layer norm along a random direction. Test items are 60 facts in both languages; a prompt counts if the clean model answers it correctly in its own language (79 prompts on 4B, 63 on 1.7B). A *full flip* means the output language changed and the answer is still correct.

<RegisterDoseCurve />

At α = 0.125, full flips reach 64.6% on 4B and 55.6% on 1.7B, and 0% under the random control (exact McNemar test, p < 10⁻⁴ on both). Re-estimating the axis on five bootstrap resamples of the parallel pairs gives 59.5 ± 2.7% on 4B and 56.2 ± 1.8% on 1.7B, and the random control stays at 0% on every seed. The figure shows where the answer survives: mostly when the output flips into Chinese. From Chinese into English it survives on the capital template and on 2 of 27 other facts.

The July version of this note read the covert subset, items whose target-language answer was absent from the clean output, as showing that the axis carries the language setting and not the content. Full flips do drop there, to 29.2% on 4B and 33.3% on 1.7B, while language flips stay at 79 to 96%. But half or more of those covert prompts are Chinese-to-English facts outside the capital template, where full flips are rare in any case, so the drop is confounded with direction. Within English to Chinese the covert prompts flip fully on 7 of 9 and 7 of 10. I no longer read that subset as separating the register from the content it conditions.

A second axis behaves the same way. Erasing a typo axis abolishes the model's ability to correct a misspelled word, reaching 0% at the full gap, while the random control leaves correction intact at α ≤ 0.5 (p < 10⁻⁴). On Gemma-2-2B both writes reproduce: typo correction survives on 79.6% of items at α = 0.5 against 100% for random, and the language axis gives 25.0% full flips at α = 0.125 with the random control at 0%. That is 39 to 45% of the Qwen rates, and it is not a knowledge gap: on items Gemma can answer in the target language it still full-flips only 27.8%.

## Swaps change the restated question

Edits along lens directions also reach beyond the next token. A band swap replaces a question's subject with another entity along the two entities' lens directions; the model answers, and then, with the swap hooks off, continues from "The exact question I was asked was:". On 1.7B the full swap flips the answer on 78.6% of 56 capital items, and the restatement reproduces the swapped question on 71.4% (no-swap baseline 0%). A lens read on the hook-free restatement passes agrees with behavior on 54 of 56 items. The rate is 58.9 to 69.6% from 4B to 14B and 83.9% on Gemma-2-2B, where the no-swap arm already reads as the swapped question on 5.4% of items. The random control flips no answer at any scale, and the effect holds on US states and on a currency relation. The restatement is generated after the answer, so the model may be reconstructing the question from its own answer; the claim is only that the restated question follows the edit.

## What I conclude now

On these models the Jacobian lens reads two things. On the content sets its hits fit next-token anticipation, and where the lens reads a computed entity at all, that holds whatever role the entity plays. What survives mouth-exclusion is content in a surface form the output does not use: English forms of the prompt's words and answer, a corrected spelling, and less often a language name. Whether the English answer is anticipation in another language, my data do not settle.

Three findings hold on their own terms. A language axis estimated from parallel sentences is causal against an amplitude-matched control. The residual stream holds more than the lens reports, since a trained probe reads the rhyme constraint where the lens reads nothing. Band swaps along lens directions change the question the model restates.

What the audit does not support is any statement about planning. The asymmetry across task families is real, but it matches what an exact-token filter removes. On these models, at 1.7B to 4B, the lens alone cannot tell planned content from unplanned. A frontier model, or Gemma-3-27B where rhyme planning has been reported, may hold representations these models lack; this bounds what the lens shows here, not what larger models do.
